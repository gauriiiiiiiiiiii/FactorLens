WHAT IS FACTORLENS?
-------------------
FactorLens is a quantitative equity factor-discovery platform. It answers the
question: "Which stock characteristics (factors) actually predict future
returns?" The app loads pre-processed price and fundamental data, engineers 15
cross-sectional factors, trains three ML models to rank stocks, builds long-
short portfolios, and validates everything with a walk-forward backtest — all
through a professional Streamlit dashboard.

Use case: quant researchers, finance students, or anyone who wants to understand
how factor investing works in practice — not theory.

  DATA SOURCES

Raw data is NOT included in the repo (deleted after processing). Processed

Original sources used to generate the processed data:

1. STOCK PRICES  —  jacksoncrow/stock-market-dataset  (Kaggle, ~522 MB)
   - ~5,884 stocks + ETFs as individual CSVs
   - Columns: Date, Open, High, Low, Close, Adj Close, Volume
   - Adj Close preferred over Close (accounts for splits and dividends)
   - 500 tickers were loaded; 264 tickers survived after merge with fundamentals

2. FUNDAMENTALS  —  cnic92/200-financial-indicators  (Kaggle, ~15 MB)
   - Annual financial statements for US stocks: 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018
   - 200+ columns originally; only 11 columns used in the merge:
     book_value, net_income, total_assets, revenue, pe_ratio,
     roe, roa, shares_outstanding, market_cap, ticker, date
   - Year inferred from filename; date set to Dec 31 of that year


================================================================================
  DATA PIPELINE (step by step)
================================================================================

STEP 1 — load_prices()  [src/loader.py]
  - Reads up to `max_tickers` CSVs from data/raw/prices/stocks/
  - Normalises all column names (lowercase, underscores, no special chars)
  - Maps flexible column names to standard names: date, ticker, open, high,
    low, close, volume
  - Drops rows where date or close is missing
  - Returns a single DataFrame with all tickers stacked

STEP 2 — load_fundamentals()  [src/loader.py]
  - Reads all 5 annual CSVs from data/raw/fundamentals/
  - Normalises column names the same way
  - Maps fundamental columns to standard names:
      market_cap, book_value, pe_ratio, pb_ratio, revenue, net_income,
      total_assets, shares_outstanding, eps, roe, roa, debt_equity,
      revenue_growth, ebitda, free_cash_flow, operating_income
  - Infers the year from filename, sets date = Dec 31 of that year
  - Returns a stacked DataFrame

STEP 3 — compute_returns()  [src/preprocess.py]
  - Calculates daily percentage return per ticker: close.pct_change()
  - Winsorises returns at 1st and 99th percentile to remove outliers
    (extreme single-day moves would dominate ML training otherwise)

STEP 4 — merge_fundamentals()  [src/preprocess.py]
  - Uses pandas merge_asof (as-of merge) to join fundamentals to prices
  - For each price date, looks backward to find the most recent annual filing
    for that ticker — this prevents look-ahead bias
  - Converts all fundamental columns to numeric (coerces bad values to NaN)

STEP 5 — build()  [src/features.py]
  - Engineers all 15 active factors (see FACTOR DEFINITIONS section below)
  - Adds return_next = next day's return (the prediction target)
  - Cross-sectionally z-scores each factor per date:
      z = (factor - mean) / std  (computed within each date's cross-section)
    This removes market-wide level effects; only relative ranks matter
  - Drops rows where any factor or the target is NaN


================================================================================
  FACTOR DEFINITIONS  (src/features.py)
================================================================================

16 factors are defined in ALL_FEATURES in src/features.py. With the current
processed dataset (jacksoncrow prices + cnic92 fundamentals), 15 are active
— roa is defined but had no data in the cnic92 dataset so it is excluded.

Active factors:
  momentum_12m, momentum_6m, momentum_3m, reversal_1m,
  volatility_3m, volatility_1m,
  size, value, profitability, quality, roe,
  growth, earnings_yield, leverage, liquidity

All active factors are cross-sectionally z-scored before use (mean=0, std=1 per date).
The available() function in features.py automatically filters out any factor
that is missing or all-NaN in the data, so the pipeline adapts to whatever
columns are present.

--- PRICE-BASED FACTORS (computed from daily price history) ---

1.  momentum_12m   = close.pct_change(252) - close.pct_change(21)
                     12-month return minus last 1-month. Excluding the recent
                     month avoids short-term reversal contaminating the signal.

2.  momentum_6m    = close.pct_change(126)
                     6-month price return.

3.  momentum_3m    = close.pct_change(63)
                     3-month price return.

4.  reversal_1m    = close.pct_change(21)
                     1-month price return. Expected to have NEGATIVE IC —
                     short-term winners tend to mean-revert next month.

5.  volatility_3m  = return.rolling(63, min_periods=20).std()
                     3-month rolling standard deviation of daily returns.

6.  volatility_1m  = return.rolling(21, min_periods=10).std()
                     1-month rolling standard deviation of daily returns.

7.  size           = log(close × shares_outstanding)
                     Log of market cap. Log transform reduces skew — a $1T
                     company should not be 1000× more "large" than a $1B company
                     in the model's eyes.

8.  liquidity      = log(1 + volume.rolling(21).mean())
                     Log of 21-day average trading volume. Measures how easily
                     the stock can be bought or sold.

--- FUNDAMENTAL FACTORS (computed from annual financial data) ---

9.  value          = book_value / market_cap
                     Book-to-market ratio. Higher = stock is cheap relative to
                     its accounting value. The classic "value" factor.

10. profitability  = net_income / total_assets
                     How much profit the company earns per unit of assets.

11. quality        = net_income / revenue
                     Net profit margin. Measures earnings quality — how much of
                     each rupee of sales actually becomes profit.

12. roe            = return on equity (taken directly from fundamentals)
                     Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity.

13. roa            = return on assets (taken directly from fundamentals)
                     Net Income ÷ Total Assets.

14. earnings_yield = 1 / pe_ratio
                     Inverse of P/E. High earnings yield = cheap stock.
                     Used instead of P/E because P/E explodes when earnings
                     are near zero; earnings yield stays bounded.

15. leverage       = total_assets / market_cap
                     How large the balance sheet is relative to market value.
                     High leverage = company is heavily debt-funded.

16. growth         = revenue.pct_change(4) per ticker
                     Annual revenue growth. pct_change(4) because fundamentals
                     are annual — 4 periods = comparing to one year ago.


================================================================================
  PORTFOLIO CONSTRUCTION  (src/portfolio.py)
================================================================================

LONG-SHORT FACTOR RETURNS
  For each factor and each date:
  - Sort all stocks by that factor's z-score
  - Long top quantile (best stocks by factor), Short bottom quantile
  - Return = mean(long portfolio return) − mean(short portfolio return)
  - n_quantiles controls the quantile size (default 5 → top/bottom 20%)
  - Result: a time series of daily factor returns

INFORMATION COEFFICIENT (IC)
  - Spearman rank correlation between factor values and next-day returns
  - Computed for each factor × each date
  - IC > 0 means the factor correctly ranked stocks (higher factor = higher return)
  - Mean IC > 0.02 is considered meaningful in practice

BACKTEST
  - Uses ML model predictions (pred column) as the ranking signal
  - Same long-short construction as above
  - Computes: cumulative return, drawdown (peak-to-trough loss)
  - No transaction costs or slippage (educational backtest)


================================================================================
  ML MODELS  (src/train.py)
================================================================================

WHY 3 MODELS?
  Each model looks for a different type of signal. Using all three and comparing
  them is how you verify the factor signal is real:
  - If all three show good IC → the factors are genuinely predictive
  - If only one model does well → it has likely overfit to noise

  LASSO         Finds which factors matter (linear, sparse)
  Random Forest Finds combinations of factors that matter together (non-linear)
  XGBoost       Usually the most accurate — best at extracting weak signals

All models predict return_next from the 15 active z-scored factors.
Train/test split: first 80% of rows (chronological, no shuffling — avoids
data leakage across time).

1. LASSO  (sklearn LassoCV)
   - Linear model with L1 regularisation
   - Automatically drives irrelevant factor weights to exactly zero
   - Cross-validates the regularisation strength over 5 folds
   - Feature importance = signed coefficients
     (positive = bullish factor, negative = contrarian / bearish factor)
   - Best answer to: "Which 3-4 factors actually matter?"

2. RANDOM FOREST  (sklearn RandomForestRegressor)
   - 400 decision trees averaged together
   - Captures non-linear relationships, e.g. "high momentum AND low volatility
     together predict returns, but individually they don't"
   - Feature importance = mean decrease in impurity (always positive, no sign)
   - Best answer to: "Are there interaction effects between factors?"

3. XGBOOST  (xgb.XGBRegressor)
   - Gradient-boosted trees: each tree corrects the errors of the previous one
   - 500 trees, learning rate 0.05, L1 + L2 regularisation to prevent overfit
   - Generally the most accurate on tabular financial data
   - Feature importance = gain-based (always positive, no sign)
   - Best answer to: "What is the strongest predictive signal we can extract?"

EVALUATION METRICS
  MSE  : Mean Squared Error — average squared prediction error (lower = better)
  R²   : Fraction of return variance explained by the model (higher = better)
         Even R² = 0.001 can be economically meaningful in return prediction
  IC   : Spearman rank correlation between predictions and actual next-day returns
         The most relevant metric — in factor investing, correct ranking matters
         more than predicting the exact magnitude of returns
         IC > 0.02 (mean) is considered practically useful


================================================================================
  FACTOR EXPOSURE CALCULATOR  (src/exposure.py)
================================================================================

Answers: "What factor tilts does my portfolio have?"

Given a portfolio (dict of ticker → weight):
- Looks up each ticker's latest factor z-scores
- Computes weighted sum of each factor's z-score across the portfolio
- Result: a Series of factor exposures sorted by absolute magnitude
- Positive exposure = portfolio tilts toward high-factor stocks
- Negative exposure = portfolio tilts away from high-factor stocks

Example: AAPL + MSFT equal-weight portfolio shows:
  momentum_12m:  +0.72   → recent winners
  size:          +1.85   → large caps
  value:         -0.63   → slightly expensive (growth tilt)

================================================================================
  HOW TO RUN
================================================================================

1. Install dependencies:
      pip install -r requirements.txt

2. Run the app:
      streamlit run app.py

3. In the sidebar, choose a model, set quantiles, and click "Run Pipeline".
   The app loads from data/processed/ automatically — no raw data needed.

TO REGENERATE FROM SCRATCH (if you have the raw Kaggle files):
   Place prices in data/raw/prices/stocks/ and fundamentals in
   data/raw/fundamentals/, then run this script once:

      python -c "
      from src.loader import load_prices, load_fundamentals
      from src.preprocess import compute_returns, merge_fundamentals
      from src.features import build, available
      from src.portfolio import factor_returns
      from src.config import PROCESSED_FEATURES, PROCESSED_FACTORS
      prices = compute_returns(load_prices(max_tickers=500))
      merged = merge_fundamentals(prices, load_fundamentals())
      df = build(merged)
      fret = factor_returns(df, available(df))
      PROCESSED_FEATURES.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
      df.to_csv(PROCESSED_FEATURES, index=False)
      fret.to_csv(PROCESSED_FACTORS)
      print('Done.')
      "

================================================================================
  FINANCIAL TERMS GLOSSARY
================================================================================

--- CORE INVESTING CONCEPTS ---

FACTOR INVESTING
  A strategy that selects stocks based on measurable characteristics (factors)
  that have historically been associated with higher returns. Examples: cheap
  stocks (value factor), recent winners (momentum factor). Instead of picking
  individual stocks, you systematically buy the best-ranked stocks by a factor
  and short the worst-ranked ones.

LONG POSITION
  Buying a stock with the expectation that its price will go up. If you are
  "long AAPL", you own AAPL shares and profit when AAPL rises.

SHORT POSITION
  Borrowing and selling a stock you don't own, expecting its price to fall.
  You buy it back later at a lower price to return it. Profit = sell price −
  repurchase price. Risk: unlimited if the stock keeps rising.

LONG-SHORT PORTFOLIO
  A market-neutral strategy where you simultaneously:
    - Long the top-ranked stocks by a factor (expected to outperform)
    - Short the bottom-ranked stocks (expected to underperform)
  The idea is that you profit from the relative difference, regardless of
  whether the overall market goes up or down. This removes "market beta".

MARKET BETA
  How much a stock moves relative to the market. Beta = 1 means the stock
  moves exactly with the market. A long-short portfolio targets beta ≈ 0
  (market neutral), so returns are driven purely by the factor, not the market.

ALPHA
  Return above and beyond what the market explains. A strategy with positive
  alpha generates excess returns after accounting for market risk. Factor
  returns are essentially a source of alpha.

QUANTILE
  A way to divide a sorted list into equal groups. If n_quantiles = 5:
    - Quantile 1 (bottom 20%): worst stocks by factor → SHORT these
    - Quantile 5 (top 20%):    best stocks by factor  → LONG these
  Long-short return = average return of top quantile − average return of bottom quantile.

ANNUAL REPORT / 10-K FILING
  A detailed yearly financial report that US public companies must file with
  the SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission). Contains income statement,
  balance sheet, and cash flow statement. The fundamentals dataset is built
  from 10-K data.

LOOK-AHEAD BIAS
  Using information in your model that would not have been available at the
  time of trading. Example: using the 2016 annual report data for trades made
  in January 2016 (report wasn't published until March 2016). This makes
  backtests look better than they really are. FactorLens avoids this with
  an as-of merge.

TRANSACTION COSTS / SLIPPAGE
  Real-world costs of trading: brokerage commissions, bid-ask spread, and
  market impact (large orders move the price against you). FactorLens does
  NOT model these — it is an educational backtest. In practice, factor
  strategies must clear their transaction costs to be profitable.

--- FINANCIAL STATEMENTS ---

INCOME STATEMENT (P&L)
  Shows what the company earned and spent over a period.
  Revenue → (minus costs) → Gross Profit → (minus expenses) → Operating Income
  → (minus interest/taxes) → Net Income

BALANCE SHEET
  A snapshot of what the company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) at a
  point in time. Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity.

CASH FLOW STATEMENT
  Tracks actual cash moving in and out — separated into:
    Operating cash flow : cash from core business
    Investing cash flow : cash from buying/selling assets (capex, acquisitions)
    Financing cash flow : cash from debt or equity issuance/buybacks

--- FUNDAMENTAL METRICS ---

REVENUE (Sales)
  Total money a company earns from selling its products or services before
  any costs are deducted. Also called "top line".

GROSS PROFIT
  Revenue minus the direct cost of making goods (Cost of Goods Sold / COGS).
  Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS

OPERATING INCOME (EBIT)
  Profit from the company's core operations, before interest and taxes.
  EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes.

NET INCOME
  The final profit after all expenses, interest, and taxes. Also called
  "bottom line". Net Income = Revenue − All Costs − Interest − Taxes.

EBITDA
  Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortisation. A proxy
  for operating cash flow. Often used in valuation multiples.

FREE CASH FLOW (FCF)
  Cash generated by the business after paying for capital expenditures (capex).
  FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures
  Considered the most "real" measure of profitability — harder to manipulate
  than net income.

EPS (Earnings Per Share)
  Net Income ÷ Number of shares outstanding. Measures profitability per share.
  EPS Diluted accounts for stock options and convertibles that could increase
  share count.

BOOK VALUE PER SHARE
  Total Shareholders' Equity ÷ Shares Outstanding. Represents the accounting
  value of one share if the company were liquidated. Used in P/B ratio.

TOTAL ASSETS
  Everything the company owns: cash, inventory, property, equipment,
  intangibles, investments. Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Equity.

TOTAL DEBT
  Short-term debt + Long-term debt. Measures how much the company has borrowed.

SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY
  What belongs to shareholders after subtracting all liabilities from assets.
  Equity = Assets − Liabilities. Also called "net worth" or "book value".

MARKET CAPITALISATION (Market Cap)
  Current stock price × total shares outstanding. Represents the total market
  value of the company. FactorLens computes this as: close × shares_outstanding.

SHARES OUTSTANDING
  The total number of shares currently held by all shareholders (including
  institutional investors, insiders, and the public).

--- VALUATION RATIOS ---

P/E RATIO (Price-to-Earnings)
  Stock Price ÷ EPS. How much investors pay for each unit of earnings.
  P/E = 20 means investors pay $20 for every $1 of annual earnings.
  High P/E = growth expectations or overvaluation.
  Low P/E = cheap stock or poor growth prospects (value stocks).

P/B RATIO (Price-to-Book)
  Stock Price ÷ Book Value per Share. Compares market price to accounting value.
  P/B < 1 means market values the company below its book value (potentially cheap).

EARNINGS YIELD
  1 ÷ P/E ratio. The inverse of P/E expressed as a percentage.
  Earnings Yield = EPS ÷ Price. High earnings yield = cheap stock.
  Used instead of P/E because it is easier to handle mathematically (P/E → ∞
  when earnings are near zero; earnings yield → 0 smoothly).

EV / EBITDA (Enterprise Value Multiple)
  Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA. A valuation ratio that includes debt.
  Enterprise Value = Market Cap + Debt − Cash.
  Used to compare companies with different capital structures.

PRICE-TO-SALES (P/S)
  Market Cap ÷ Revenue. Useful when earnings are negative (e.g. early-stage
  companies) because revenue is always positive.

--- PROFITABILITY RATIOS ---

ROE (Return on Equity)
  Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity. Measures how efficiently management uses
  shareholders' money to generate profit. ROE = 20% means $20 profit per $100
  of equity. High ROE is a sign of a quality company.

ROA (Return on Assets)
  Net Income ÷ Total Assets. Measures how efficiently the company uses all its
  assets (including debt-funded ones). Less affected by leverage than ROE.

ROIC (Return on Invested Capital)
  Net Operating Profit After Tax ÷ Invested Capital. Measures returns on all
  capital deployed (equity + debt). The gold standard of profitability metrics.

PROFIT MARGIN (Net Profit Margin)
  Net Income ÷ Revenue. What percentage of sales becomes actual profit.
  Margin = 10% means $10 of every $100 in sales flows to the bottom line.

GROSS MARGIN
  Gross Profit ÷ Revenue. What's left after paying direct production costs.
  High gross margins indicate pricing power (e.g. software companies).

OPERATING MARGIN (EBIT Margin)
  Operating Income ÷ Revenue. Efficiency of core operations before financing costs.

--- LEVERAGE / DEBT RATIOS ---

DEBT-TO-EQUITY RATIO
  Total Debt ÷ Shareholders' Equity. Measures financial leverage.
  High ratio = company relies heavily on debt (higher risk, but potentially
  higher returns if the business earns more than its interest cost).

DEBT-TO-ASSETS RATIO
  Total Debt ÷ Total Assets. Fraction of assets funded by borrowing.

INTEREST COVERAGE RATIO
  EBIT ÷ Interest Expense. How many times the company can pay its interest
  from operating income. Below 1.5x is considered risky.

NET DEBT
  Total Debt − Cash & Cash Equivalents. The "real" debt burden after using
  available cash to repay. Negative net debt = more cash than debt (net cash).

--- LIQUIDITY RATIOS ---

CURRENT RATIO
  Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities. Ability to pay short-term obligations.
  Above 1 means current assets cover current liabilities. Below 1 = potential
  liquidity problem.

QUICK RATIO (Acid Test)
  (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities. More conservative than
  current ratio — excludes inventory which may be hard to sell quickly.

CASH RATIO
  Cash ÷ Current Liabilities. The most conservative liquidity measure.

--- GROWTH METRICS ---

REVENUE GROWTH
  (Current Revenue − Prior Revenue) ÷ Prior Revenue. How fast the company
  is growing its top line. In FactorLens: annual revenue pct_change(4) because
  fundamentals are reported annually.

EPS GROWTH
  Growth in earnings per share. Sustainable EPS growth + high ROE = compounding
  machine (Warren Buffett's framework).

DIVIDEND PER SHARE / DIVIDEND YIELD
  Dividend Per Share ÷ Stock Price. The cash income return from holding the stock.
  Dividend Yield = 4% means you earn $4 per $100 invested annually in dividends.

--- PRICE / MOMENTUM METRICS ---

MOMENTUM
  The tendency of recent winners to keep winning and recent losers to keep losing
  over the next 3–12 months. One of the most robust factors in finance (documented
  across 200+ years of market data). FactorLens uses 12m, 6m, 3m variants.

SHORT-TERM REVERSAL
  The 1-month version of momentum actually shows NEGATIVE predictability —
  stocks that went up last month tend to slightly underperform next month.
  This is the reversal_1m factor (expected negative IC).

VOLATILITY
  The standard deviation of daily returns over a rolling window. High-volatility
  stocks are riskier. The "low volatility anomaly" shows that low-vol stocks
  often outperform on a risk-adjusted basis — counterintuitive to theory.

--- PORTFOLIO PERFORMANCE METRICS ---

CUMULATIVE RETURN
  The total compounded return of a strategy from start to end.
  Cumulative = (1+r1) × (1+r2) × ... × (1+rn) − 1
  Expressed as a percentage of initial investment.

SHARPE RATIO
  (Mean Return − Risk-Free Rate) ÷ Standard Deviation of Returns.
  Measures return per unit of risk. FactorLens simplifies: no risk-free rate
  subtracted (assumes it's near zero). Annualised by multiplying by √252
  (there are ~252 trading days per year). Sharpe > 1 is generally good.

MAX DRAWDOWN
  The largest peak-to-trough decline in the cumulative return series.
  If portfolio grew to $130 then fell to $90, max drawdown = −30.8%.
  Measures the worst-case loss an investor could have experienced.

INFORMATION COEFFICIENT (IC)
  Spearman rank correlation between predicted factor values and actual next-day
  returns. Ranges from −1 to +1.
    IC > 0   : factor correctly ranked stocks (good signal)
    IC = 0   : factor has no predictive power (random)
    IC < 0   : factor is a contrarian indicator
  Mean IC > 0.02 is considered practically meaningful in institutional quant.

SPEARMAN RANK CORRELATION
  Measures how well the relationship between two variables is described by a
  monotonic function (not necessarily linear). Converts values to ranks first,
  then computes Pearson correlation of the ranks. Robust to outliers — important
  because stock returns have fat tails.

INFORMATION RATIO (IR)
  Mean IC ÷ Standard Deviation of IC. Measures consistency of the signal.
  High IC mean is good; high IC volatility is bad (unstable factor).

ANNUALISATION (√252 scaling)
  Daily Sharpe × √252 = annual Sharpe.
  Daily return × 252 = annual return.
  Daily volatility × √252 = annual volatility.
  Based on approximately 252 trading days per year in US markets.

MARKET REGIME
  The current "state" of the market. FactorLens classifies each day as:
    Risk-On  🟢 : above-median returns AND below-median volatility (good times)
    Risk-Off 🔴 : below-median returns AND above-median volatility (bad times)
    Mixed    🟡 : all other combinations
  Different factors work better in different regimes.

--- STATISTICAL / TECHNICAL TERMS ---

WINSORISATION
  Capping extreme values at a percentile rather than removing them.
  In FactorLens: returns below 1st percentile are set to the 1st percentile
  value; returns above 99th percentile are capped at the 99th percentile.
  Prevents a single extreme event from dominating model training.

CROSS-SECTIONAL Z-SCORE
  Standardising a factor's values across all stocks on a given date.
  Z = (value − mean of all stocks today) ÷ (std dev of all stocks today)
  Ensures the factor has mean=0 and std=1 every day, making it comparable
  across different time periods and removing market-level trends.

AS-OF MERGE (merge_asof)
  A type of join that matches each row in table A to the most recent preceding
  row in table B. Used to join annual fundamentals to daily prices without
  look-ahead bias: a price on 2016-03-15 gets the 2015-12-31 annual data
  (the most recent filing actually available on that date).

STANDARD DEVIATION
  Measures the spread of values around the mean. In finance, it measures risk
  (how much returns deviate from their average). σ = √( Σ(x−μ)² / n )

MSE (Mean Squared Error)
  Average of squared prediction errors. MSE = mean( (predicted − actual)² )
  Lower is better. Penalises large errors heavily due to squaring.

R² (R-Squared / Coefficient of Determination)
  Fraction of variance in the target (return_next) explained by the model.
  R² = 1: perfect prediction. R² = 0: model is no better than predicting the mean.
  R² < 0: model is worse than predicting the mean.
  In return prediction, even R² = 0.001 can be economically significant.

L1 REGULARISATION (Lasso)
  Adds a penalty = λ × Σ|coefficient| to the loss function.
  Drives small coefficients to exactly zero, producing sparse models.
  Automatically performs feature selection — irrelevant factors get zero weight.

L2 REGULARISATION (Ridge)
  Adds a penalty = λ × Σ(coefficient²). Shrinks all coefficients toward zero
  but rarely sets them exactly to zero. XGBoost uses reg_lambda for L2.


================================================================================
  DESIGN DECISIONS
================================================================================

WHY as-of merge for fundamentals?
  Annual reports are released months after year-end. Using a backward as-of
  merge ensures we never use financial data before it was publicly available.
  This prevents look-ahead bias — one of the most common mistakes in backtesting.

WHY winsorise returns?
  A single stock can move ±50% in a day (earnings, acquisition, etc.).
  Without winsorisation these outliers would dominate the MSE loss function
  and distort model training.

WHY cross-sectional z-score?
  Factor levels change over time (e.g. average P/E was 15x in 2014, 25x in 2018).
  Z-scoring within each date removes this time-series drift and makes factors
  comparable across different market environments.

WHY chronological train/test split?
  Random shuffling would leak future information into training. Using the first
  80% of time for training and the last 20% for testing mirrors real deployment.

WHY Spearman IC instead of Pearson correlation?
  Returns are heavy-tailed. Spearman rank correlation is robust to outliers
  and directly measures what matters: did we rank stocks in the right order?
